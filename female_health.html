Why girls in the Sundarbans are leaving school early

Child marriage and motherhood between ages 10 and 19 harm girls' bodies, minds and futures, and trap families in poverty and ill health. Four forces keep the cycle going.

The Sundarbans delta is cut off by rivers and poor roads. Basic health care and schools are hard to reach.

Serious risks for mothers and newborns

Early pregnancy raises the risk of anaemia, pre-eclampsia and eclampsia, premature rupture of membranes, severe bleeding and cervical cancer. Newborns face preterm birth, low birth weight, low Apgar scores, stillbirth and death in the first weeks of life.

Distance, water and hunger

River barriers and missing roads keep girls away from clinics and hospitals. Daily contact with saline water causes chronic skin disease, and long-term malnutrition adds to the harm.

Silence around family planning

Taboos and secrecy leave girls without knowledge of contraception or their legal rights under the Medical Termination of Pregnancy (MTP) Act. Many turn to unsafe abortions.

Social pressure and exploitation

Poverty, pressure to continue the family line, religious misconceptions and limited schooling push families toward early marriage. Substance abuse, sexual violence and exposure to pornography among minors raise the danger further.

Two projects, one village-level system

Lakshmirupa empowers women and adolescent girls. Mayer Anchal looks after mothers and children. Together they connect district and block offices to every village through female doctors, nurses, ASHA and Anganwadi workers, and five educated local girls per village.

How support reaches an island household

A four-level delivery model carries policy and medical expertise from the block office to the courtyard.

  1. Government and NGOs

    District and block authorities with local NGOs supply funding, legal backing, medical supplies and training resources.

  2. Village health team

    Trained female doctors, nurses, ASHA and Anganwadi workers hold clinics and follow up on care.

  3. Five peer leaders

    Educated local girls who guide daily routines and act as trusted bridges to health workers.

  4. Mothers and daughters

    Open courtyard meetings (Uthon Boithok) make reproductive health and legal rights ordinary topics.

Why it suits the Sundarbans

Works through monsoon and river disruption

Support lives inside each village, so it continues when boats and roads stop.

Earns trust with local voices

Mothers join the conversation, and female doctors and local peers replace unfamiliar outsiders.

Low cost, low infrastructure

Yoga, safe drinking water and iron and vitamin C-rich local food need little equipment and scale easily.

Tackles causes together

Health care, income and schooling together show families that daughters can be earners, not burdens.

Common questions

Short answers about the programme, the law and how to take part.

What do Lakshmirupa and Mayer Anchal each do?

Lakshmirupa empowers women and adolescent girls. Mayer Anchal looks after the health of mothers and children. They run together through one village-level system.

Who are the peer leaders?

Five educated local girls in each village. They guide daily nutrition and yoga sessions and connect girls with health workers. Each receives a stipend of ₹1,500 a month.

What does the MTP Act allow?

Abortion is legal up to 20 weeks, from 20 to 24 weeks in special cases, and beyond 24 weeks for severe fetal abnormalities. The programme directs girls to government-approved hospitals with follow-up care.

Where can I report abuse or a forced marriage?

Contact the National Emergency Helpline (999) or the Women’s Affairs department. Villages are linked to both, and the POCSO Act covers abuse of minors.

Can my organisation partner on the pilot?

Yes. Government offices, NGOs, health workers and funders can write through the contact form below.
